✨ What We Do
We build multimodal biological research evaluations that test and improve models' ability to visually interpret and reason about slides and specimens in the field of Histopathology, Cytology, Hematology, Molecular imaging, Agricultural microbiology, and more. A core design principle is realism: our images include artifacts, contamination, signal Vs noise, and failed experiments that experienced researchers recognize immediately.

Fellows produce two deliverable formats per assay type: Visual Question Answering (VQA) pairs with multiple-choice distractors, and comprehensive text descriptions covering modality, quality, and visible artifacts.

Understand the tasking pipeline
Once you submit a task it moves through a pipeline of automated checks and human reviews before it's ready to deliver. At several of these stages, the task can bounce back to you for a fix. This page walks through every stage so you know exactly where and why your task might return.

The seven stages
01
Stage 1 — Attempt stage
Automated checks
We have several in-task quality checkers for the prompt, image description, model failures, science judge and distractor format. These checks flag any issues that must be fixed in the task itself before submission. Your prompt must also require at least three levels of reasoning to answer. If you disagree with a flag, you must provide a clear written justification for why you disagree. In addition, any submitted task must have both responder models fail in order to be submitted.

There are many steps involved in this stage. They are described in detail in the Overview & Lifecycle section below.

02
Stage 2 — Justification Check (Holding)
Justification review
Can return to you
Your disagreements/rebuttals to the in-task quality checkers are reviewed here. If a justification is determined to be insufficient, the task is returned to you to either provide a stronger justification or modify the task to remove the Science Judge flag. If there is no resolution after two attempts, you will be directed to fill out a dispute form that will be reviewed by a human expert.

03
Stage 3 — Holding (Pass@) - First model dispatch
Model check
Can return to you
We run two models against the task again. At least 1 of 2 must fail for the task to advance. If not, the task is returned to you.

04
Stage 4 — Review 1
Human review
Can return to you
A human reviewer inspects the task. Any issues found here are returned to you. Tasks with no issues pass through to the next stage.

05
Stage 5 — Holding (Final Pass@) - Second model dispatch
Quality signal
Can return to you
We run four model checks. At least 2 of 4 must fail for the task to pass. If not, the task is returned to you.

06
Stage 6 — Review 2
Human QC
Can return to you
A sampled portion of tasks receives a second human QC pass. If issues are found here, the task can still be returned to you for a final fix.

07
Stage 7 — Ready to deliver
Final
The task has cleared every automated and human checkpoint. It is finalized and delivered to the customer. Nothing comes back to you from this stage.

You will be paid for tasks that make it to this stage.

From where can a task come back to you?
Note
Tasks returned from the automated stages (2,3,5) will have comments at the top of the page and tasks returned from the manual review stages (4,6)  will have comments on the right that need to be addressed.

Five return points
Stage 2 — Justification Check (Holding): your justification for a Science Judge flag is judged insufficient.
Stage 3 — Holding (Pass@) - First model dispatch: fewer than 1 of 2 models fail.
Stage 4 — Review 1: a human reviewer flags issues.
Stage 5 — Holding (Final Pass@) - Second model dispatch: fewer than 2 of 4 models fail.
Stage 6 — Review 2: a sampled QC pass surfaces remaining issues.
Everywhere else, the pipeline moves forward on its own. The goal of every checklist and every step in the workflow is to get your task through all seven stages on the first pass — the fewer returns, the faster the task is delivered and the less rework for you.

What makes a good image for the Multimodal Task

For the Engineering and CS domain, images will primarily be diagrams -- e.g., of beams, simple structures, flow networks, batch reactor networks, circuit/logic diagrams, road networks and intersections/corridors, etc.

The maximum number of images that can be used per task is 5.
When adding multiple images, the numbering/naming scheme of the images MUST match how you refer to them in the prompt.
Images must be JPEG or PNG format.
Make sure that the image is at a resolution that allows a HUMAN reviewer to obtain the correct answer. Do not use screenshots when you are in Dark Mode making text difficult to read (particularly colored text).
What makes a good image
Real, not idealized
Try to make your diagrams/images as realistic as possible, within the constraints of a request that could reasonably be solved in a single shot without extensive calculation. (E.g., focus on structural members and simple structures, not comprehensive engineering plans.)

Image-dependent reasoning
The image must contain the evidence needed to answer the prompt. Focus on geometry/topology -- provide the beam diagram, flow network, etc + any relevant measurements in the image.

Labels preserved
Keep all axis labels, lane labels, scale bars, and color legends. These are part of the visual reasoning the task tests.

Original resolution
No screenshots of screenshots. Only PNG and JPEG are allowed. Do not enhance, sharpen, or color-correct beyond the original capture.

Adversarial potential
The image should expose specific failure modes: axis confusion, miscounting in dense fields, magnification mismatches, signal-vs-artifact discrimination.

Minimum image annotation
Don't use images with unnecessary annotations, and don't add annotations that help the model.

✍️ Writing the Prompt
A good prompt is a question that an expert can answer confidently from the image and that a strong vision-language model gets wrong, with a clear chain of reasoning available to the human grader.


Required Properties
Click to collapse
Image-dependent. The question must require reading the image. If it can be answered from the text of the prompt alone, it is not appropriate.
Specific and unambiguous. Spell out conventions (e.g. "state the answer to 3 significant figures", "count only items fully inside the marked region"). For prompts that require reading/estimating values off of a graph, you must specify the rounding convention (e.g. "for values read off of the graph, round up to the nearest tick mark")
Expert-grade. Require domain knowledge: beam theory, transport processes (at a graduate level), solid/continuum mechanics, advanced circuit analysis, etc.
Single canonical answer. The prompt should have a single verifiable answer that any expert in the field would be able to arrive to. Avoid open-ended questions.

Reading values from figures

Unambiguous answers require unambiguous data extraction from images (diagrams, plots, etc.). For plots, this means you must specify exactly how to read values — e.g. to the nearest tick-mark, rounded up to the next tick-mark, etc. Think of it like this: if a reviewer, who is not going to break out a straight edge to evaluate your plot, is uncertain of the exact value to be extracted from the plot, the prompt is ambiguous.

Appropriate failure modes

Recall that we do not care about downstream reasoning errors (i.e., those that come after the image interpretation step) for this project. This implies two things:

You do not have to make calculation-intensive prompts.
Making extremely complex prompts will only waste your time because you have to solve the problem yourself to provide the final answer. The prompts must be real engineering questions that involve engineering-specific domain knowledge, but they do not have to be mathematically complex.
Your images should be geared towards inducing common failure modes in image interpretation. For us, these are:
Spatial relationships. Models are bad at spatial reasoning generally; complex geometric/spatial relationships confuse the model and induce failures.
Topological relationships. Models are terrible at understanding topological relationships (e.g. what objects are connected, like locations in a road network). Even moderately complex graphs (in the mathematical sense) — like those especially relevant for computer, electrical, and transportation engineering — can induce model failure.
Axis values.
Recall we don't want OCR (optical character recognition) errors. For example, if the model correctly identifies the closest tick mark but reads it as "14" instead of the correct value of "1.4," this is not a valid error.
However, if the model incorrectly interprets a plot, leading it to misidentify the correct tick mark, this is a valid error. In general, we want to avoid these types of errors and focus on the other two.
What is an unambiguous prompt?

An unambiguous prompt is one for which an engineer in the same subdomain (though likely not in the same exact specialization) can independently calculate and arrive at the same numerical answer.

Some things that introduce ambiguity:

Not specifying modeling choices when different valid approaches or approximations exist. If you are using a particular modeling assumption, you must state that in the prompt (unless there is absolutely no ambiguity in the modeling assumption that must be applied). If you'd like a specific approximation to be used, that does not necessarily have to be explicitly stated, but it should be clear from the scenario and given parameter values that the approximation is appropriate.
Not specifying constants.
Not specifying units for the final answer.
Not specifying significant figures for the final answer. Be sure the number you request is consistent with the values given in the prompt (for example, don't request three significant figures if everything in your prompt is given to one). Do not request more than 3 significant figures.
Requesting an equation or symbolic answer and not specifying all the variables (including physical constants) that appear in the requested answer.
Appropriate level of calculation

The calculations involved in your prompt must reasonably be able to be done by hand (with the aid of a non-programmable graphing calculator). The models do not have access to external tools like Python. It is OK to ask the model to obtain an answer to a given precision using, e.g., an iterative procedure, a Fourier series, or a simple numerical procedure such as finding the roots of an algebraic equation. However, the number of series terms or iterations needed to achieve the desired precision must not require a computer. There is no hard limit, but, for example, five iterations or terms is acceptable because it can reasonably be done by hand, while 100 is not. Use your best judgment.

Additionally, if the models can reasonably use different iterative or approximation techniques that will lead to minor differences in the final answer, the method you want to be used should be specified to ensure the final answer is not ambiguous.

Interpreting which channel needs adjustment in a multi-panel scatter plot.

Magnification and scale interpretation
Inferring incorrectly across image series at different magnifications.

Prompt length is limited to 2000 characters.

The model's knowledge cutoff is December 31st 2025. Do not submit prompts that require knowledge past this date.

A prompt that depends on information published after this date will fail because the model has not seen it, not because it misread the image/makes a reasoning error — which is not the failure mode this project collects.


General Image Description
Click to collapse
The general image description is used while training future models and is not used by the current models to try to answer your question. It needs to be detailed and contain the appropriate information so that an expert can answer your prompt with just the prompt and general image description alone while not containing the answer in the general image description.

It should start with a rough general description of the image, and then continuously get more and more specific by concentrating on the parts of the image that are needed to find the final answer.

It should describe the image. It does not need to state what is not in the image.


Step-by-Step Solution
Click to collapse
The step-by-step is the bridge between the image and the GTFA. A grader should be able to follow it without re-deriving the engineering from scratch.

Number steps. Use Step 1, Step 2, … (or 1), 2), …) — be consistent within a task.
Start from the image. The first one or two steps should anchor the answer in concrete visual evidence ("Note that a tertiary bypass connects the two arterials in the road network…", "Determine the load condition from the beam diagram…").
Bring in the engineering explicitly. Name the principle being used ("The Rayleigh number indicates the onset of convection under this thermal loading…", "The solution is dilute, and ionic correlations can be neglected, making Nernst-Planck the correct framework to adopt for…").
Show the calculations. All symbolic manipulations should be shown and/or explained. You don't have to spell out every step of each arithmetic calculation, but plug the numbers into the formulas and give the result.
Close with the answer. The final step should resolve to the GTFA verbatim.

Distractors
Click to collapse
Provide five distractors.
None of the distractors can be the GTFA.
Each distractor must be unique (i.e. do not duplicate any of the distractors).
Every distractor should be plausible to a non-expert and dismissable by an expert. Distractors that are obviously wrong waste a slot and lower the difficulty.
Lean on common reasoning errors: axis flips, miscounts off by one bin, plausible-sounding alternative explanations that fail on a specific detail.
Anchor distractors to errors models tend to make. If you've already seen a model fail in a related task, mine that failure for distractor ideas.

 Quality Bar & Common Pitfalls
🎯 Hold the Bar — every task must satisfy:
A peer in the same engineering discipline can answer the question from the image without seeing your solution.
The step-by-step solution, read alone, justifies the GTFA from the image plus prompt.
Each distractor maps to a specific plausible error.
The model fails, and you can name the failure mode in one sentence.
Image Description must be sufficient to answer the prompt without the image.
Common Pitfalls
1
Writing the prompt as an explicit multiple-choice question
Explicit multiple-choice prompts are disallowed. Do not present the responder with a list of terms, or a set of labelled images/panels, and ask them to pick one option. The prompt must ask for the answer directly, with a single canonical answer the responder has to derive from the image.

"Which of these is the closest to the necessary bias resistance to ensure the correct truth relation for this logic gate? A) 10 V, B) 15 V, C) 20 V, D) 25 V."

"Determine the minimum bias voltage necessary to ensure 90% adherence to the target truth table on average."

2
Asking a stacked question
A prompt must ask a single question. Do not bundle two different questions into one — whether they are performed on the same image or across several images. If two distinct analyses are needed, split them into separate tasks.

"Determine the maximum compressive stress and the deflection where the maximum compressive stress occurs."

"Determine the deflection at the point of maximum compressive stress."

3
Writing a pure counting question
Counting can be part of a task, but it cannot be the whole task. A prompt whose only demand is "how many X are visible" is disallowed — the count must feed a further judgement, classification, or quantitative conclusion the responder has to derive from the image.

"How many pinned connections are present in the image?"

"Determine the force and moment reactions at the leftmost pinned connection."

4
Mixing observation and interpretation in the early solution steps
The opening steps should record what is visible. Keep interpretive claims out of them and place the reasoning in the later steps of the step-by-step solution.

"It is clear from Image 1 that the flow resistance is dominated by the form drag of the large objects on the bed."

"Image 1 indicates the presence of large objects on the channel bed."

5
Distractors an undergraduate would dismiss in five seconds
Raise the floor on plausibility. Every distractor must be plausible to a non-expert and dismissable only by an expert. Anchor them to specific reasoning errors models actually make.

6
Skipping the calculation in the step-by-step
Algebraic manipulations and arithmetic calculations must be shown explicitly. It is OK to motivate simple manipulations with words ("Squaring both sides of the equation, we obtain..."), but multistep manipulations must be shown explicitly.

7
Delivering a task the model got right
We are building an adversarial set; passing tasks are not the deliverable. If you cannot make it fail, redesign the prompt to target a tighter failure mode, swap in a harder image, or retire the task.

8
External images that turn out to be license-restricted
Licensing is the single most common reason a delivered task is rejected. Verify rights before authoring the task around the image. If you cannot find clear license info, treat it as All Rights Reserved and exclude.

9
Assuming open access means free to reuse
Free reading access does not imply commercial reuse rights. Always verify the specific Creative Commons designation. Image license can also differ from paper license — check the figure caption.

10
Cropping out on-image critical annotations
Preserve all axis labels, scale bars, color legends, etc. These are part of the visual reasoning that the task tests.

Common Engineering and CS Pitfalls
1
Not specifying the number of significant figures in the final answer
Numerical answers must be machine-verifiable. This requires that the format of the final answer you provide be directly comparable to the model output. The number of significant figures must therefore be specified.

For example, if you provide an answer of 0.849 (to three significant figures), we want to compare directly to a model answer with three significant figures, rather than, e.g., 0.85 or 0.8 or 0.8492.

2
Not specifying the units of the final answer
Units are ultimately arbitrary, and they therefore must be specified in the prompt, even if it seems obvious what units should be used for the final answer.

For numerical answers, your prompt must end with the following statement: “The answer should be expressed in [UNITS]. Report your final answer as a [1, 2, or 3] significant figure number without units. Any intermediate calculations should be carried out to 6 significant figures. All unstated fundamental constants should be used to 4 significant figures.”

Replace [UNITS] with the appropriate units for the final answer, and replace [1, 2, or 3] with the requested number of significant figures (no more than three).

3
Writing a prompt that is not image-dependent
Figures cannot be mere illustrations. They must be indispensable to answer the prompt!

4
Requesting more than 2-3 significant figures in your final answer
Requesting excessive precision in the final answer often leads to discrepancies due to intermediate truncation and numerical inaccuracies. Our goal is to test the reasoning of the model, not its ability to retain perfect numerical accuracy throughout a calculation.

5
Not specifying all approximations to be made or all numerical procedures to be used if there are reasonable alternatives
If the model can reasonably make different assumptions or apply different numerical solution procedures to arrive at a final answer that disagrees only slightly from the one you've provided, you must provide the assumptions/procedures to be used.

For example, if a few iterations of a numerical procedure are required to calculate the answer converged to the requested precision, and the model instead uses an approximation that leads to a slightly different answer, this creates ambiguity in the final answer and is not a valid reasoning error.

You should specify that the answer should be fully converged to the requested precision and/or the numerical procedure to be used and the number of iterations (if applicable).

6
Not specifying all modeling assumptions
If different modeling assumptions can reasonably be made to solve the problem, you must specify exactly which one to use.

This issue is particularly common when semi-empirical or empirical relations must be used.

7
Requesting answers that require excessive numerical computation
The model has no access to outside tools, so the problems you pose to the model must be reasonably solvable by hand with a non-programmable graphing calculator.

Remember, calculation mistakes are not valid reasoning failures, so there is no point in posing numerically involved calculations to the model.

It is acceptable if, e.g., the solution requires a few iterations of a numerical procedure or a few terms in a Fourier series to arrive at a numerically converged answer, but it should not go beyond what a human could reasonably do by hand.

📌 Special Cases
Typos and grammatical errors in the prompt — Only send back tasks for this issue if the errors alter the meaning of the prompt and can lead to incorrect answers, or if there are a significant number of typos and grammatical errors that make the prompt look very poor quality.
Stacked questions — If a prompt asks multiple questions that require compounded answers, it is considered a stacked question and is not allowed (e.g., "What is the antibiotic-resistant organism in the left plate, and what is the gram-positive bacterium in the middle plate?"). A prompt must ask a single question with a single unambiguous answer. When in doubt, ask in the #lumiere-reviewer channel.
